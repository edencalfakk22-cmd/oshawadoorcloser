There are two legitimate ways to hold a door open in an Oshawa building, and neither of them is a wedge. The first is a hold-open device — a magnetic holder, or a closer with an integral electromagnetic hold-open — wired so it releases the door on a fire alarm signal. That is how a cross-corridor or vestibule door in an apartment block, a long-term care home, a school or a hospital wing can stand open for traffic and still close when it matters.

The second is a low-energy power operator: the push-plate operators on barrier-free entrances, washrooms and accessible suites. Those are a genuine accessibility feature under Ontario’s barrier-free requirements, and when the operator dies the door reverts to whatever the closer behind it is set at — which is exactly when an entrance that was fine in October becomes unusable for someone in a chair in January.

We install and service both: magnetic holders and alarm interfaces, closer-holder combination units, push plates, wireless actuators, operator arms and control boxes, and the closer behind the operator so the door still behaves sensibly when the power is out or the board has failed.

FAQs

Hold-Opens & Automatic Operators — your questions

Is a magnetic hold-open allowed on a fire door?

Yes, when it is tied into the fire alarm so the door releases and self-closes on a signal. That is the compliant alternative to propping. What is not allowed is a doorstop, a wedge or a hook holding a rated door open with nothing to release it.

The accessible operator at our recreation centre has stopped working. Repair or replace?

Usually repair. Most failures are the actuator, the control board, a wiring fault or a worn operator arm rather than the whole unit, and all of those are parts. We diagnose on site and replace the entire operator only where the parts genuinely no longer exist or the unit has been damaged past sense.

What happens to the automatic door in a power failure?

It reverts to the closer behind it, which is exactly why that closer has to be set properly instead of left slack because the operator normally does the work. On a barrier-free entrance we also check the manual opening force, because a door that is fine with the operator running can be unusable without it.
